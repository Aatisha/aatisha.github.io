← Work / Amazon Shopbop

Helping shoppers find their style, one “this or that” at a time.

Shopbop, an Amazon company, wanted shoppers to engage with its catalogue, not just scroll it. With Shopbop’s team as our partner, I designed a ranking game: pick the piece you like more, ten times, and get your own top ten, recommendations and a live leaderboard of what everyone is choosing. Then I helped build it.

01 · Context

A great catalogue doesn’t sell itself anymore.

Shopbop has sold designer fashion online since 1999 and has been part of Amazon since 2006. Its catalogue was strong, but shopping it was a solitary scroll. The team wanted something social and playful that would bring shoppers back and get them talking about the brand, especially younger shoppers.

  1. EngagementGive shoppers a reason to interact with products, not just browse them.
  2. Brand awarenessSomething people would share, and come back to as fashion changes.
  3. A younger audienceReach Gen Z shoppers, who expect shopping to feel like play.

02 · Problem

Too many options, and no sense of what suits you.

Mapping a shopper’s journey showed where it went wrong. The mood dropped as soon as she started filtering, stayed low while she looked for guidance, and even after buying, she wasn’t sure she’d chosen well. The catalogue wasn’t the problem. Choosing from it was.

“There are so many options here. I don’t know where to start.”Shopper, journey mapping

03 · Research

I talked to the business and the shoppers, then looked at who else was trying.

  1. Stakeholder interviewsTwo people from Shopbop, on goals, audience, brand fit and constraints
  2. Shopper interviewsCollege students, a target group, on how they find styles and trends
  3. Competitive reviewASOS, Zalando and Revolve, then affinity mapping to bring it together

The business wanted something that moves with fashion

Shopbop wasn’t asking for another feed. They wanted a feature that changes as fast as fashion does, and that makes people want to talk about the products.

“We want to create something that will engage our customers with the constantly changing fashion industry.”Shopbop stakeholder

Shoppers wanted a second opinion

The students loved fashion, but didn’t trust their own styling. They wanted to know what was popular and what would suit them, in something simple enough to keep their attention.

“I’m not great at styling outfits, so it would be helpful to see what’s trending and get recommendations.”College student

Competitors showed trends. No one let shoppers make them.

  • ASOSInspiration, but overwhelmingCurated edits and a Gen Z look, on top of a catalogue that can feel daunting.
  • ZalandoPersonal, but passiveStrong recommendations and filters, and nothing for shoppers to actually do.
  • RevolveTrendy, but top-downTrends come from influencers, not from the people shopping.

None of them let shoppers interact with products or shape the trends. That was the opening: trends made by the community, and recommendations earned by playing.

04 · Insights

People can’t pick from a thousand things. They can always pick from two.

Affinity mapping the interviews and the competitive review left three insights, and they became the three parts of the product.

  1. Comparison is easier than choosing

    Picking between two items is quick and confident, where a full catalogue stalls people.

  2. People trust the crowd

    Seeing what others are choosing gives shoppers the validation they were missing.

  3. Personal beats popular

    Recommendations matter most when they come from your own choices.

How might wehelp shoppers discover and express their style, in a way that feels like play and still leads to the bag?

05 · Decisions

Five decisions that shaped the game.

I sketched the main screens, mapped the flow and tested mid-fidelity wireframes with shoppers before going to high fidelity. Each decision below traces back to an insight or that test.

Decision 1 From insight 1

Ask “this or that?”, not “what do you like?”

Options
A style quiz, star ratings on products, or a swipe feed of single items.
Chose
Pairwise ranking: two items side by side, one tap to pick a favourite, ten rounds to a personal top ten.
Why
Quizzes ask people to describe a style they can’t name. Comparing two things is easy, and every pick is a clean signal for ranking and recommendations.

Decision 2 From insight 1

Narrow the field before the first comparison.

Tension
Random pairs from the whole catalogue, a dress against a pair of boots, say nothing about taste.
Chose
Two quick choices before ranking: who you’re shopping for, then a category such as denim, skirts, jackets or dresses.
Why
Comparisons only mean something when like is compared with like. Two taps up front make every pick after them count.

Decision 3 From testing

Give the game rules, a finish line and a way out.

Testing showed
In the wireframes, shoppers weren’t sure how ranking worked, how long it would take, or what to do when they liked neither item.
Chose
Short instructions beside the game, a progress bar to ten, a Pass button for pairs you don’t like, and bigger product images.
Why
A game needs a visible end to be worth starting. And forcing a pick between two items you dislike gives bad data, not a preference.
Wireframe After testing

Decision 4 From insight 2

Let everyone’s picks decide what’s trending.

Tension
Competitors’ trends came from editors and influencers. Shoppers wanted to know what people like them were choosing.
Chose
A leaderboard per category, scored from every shopper’s picks with an Elo rating and updated as people play, top three called out.
Why
It turns each private choice into something public. The trends move with the community, which was exactly what Shopbop asked for.

Decision 5 From the business goal

End every game somewhere you can shop.

Tension
A game can be fun and still lead nowhere. Engagement alone wouldn’t justify the feature for Shopbop.
Chose
Every ranked item, recommendation and trend links straight to its product page, and adding to the bag suggests more pieces like it.
Why
The game is the way in, not the destination. Play builds the taste profile; the product page and bag turn it into sales.

06 · Craft

New feature, familiar store.

  • Built on Shopbop’s brand

    I studied Shopbop’s site and design system and built a style guide from their palette, so the game feels like part of the store.

  • Sketch, flow, wireframe

    Paper sketches of every main screen, a flow for each task, then mid-fidelity wireframes to test before any polish.

  • Designed, then built

    I also built and deployed the front end on AWS CloudFront, which kept the design honest about what the data could support.

07 · Outcome

A working way to shop by choosing, built with Amazon’s Shopbop team.

We designed, built and deployed the platform in a semester, and presented it to the Shopbop team. Shoppers can rank in any category, see a leaderboard shaped by everyone’s choices, and go from a recommendation to the bag in a couple of taps.

  • 3connected features: rank, trend, recommend
  • 1flow from play to product page to bag
  • Livefront end deployed on AWS CloudFront

08 · Reflection

What I’d take to the next project.

  1. Play has to earn its place. A game is easy to make fun. Making every round useful, to the shopper and to the business, was the real design work.
  2. Building it changed the design. Writing the front end showed me where a screen asked for data we didn’t have, and I simplified before it became a problem.
  3. Next, I’d go mobile-first and measure it. This-or-that belongs on a phone. I’d test there with more shoppers, and track how many picks lead to a product page and the bag.
