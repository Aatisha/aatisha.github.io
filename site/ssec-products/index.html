← Work / SSEC, UW–Madison

Taking polar climate data out of server folders, and into one place to study it.

Scientists at UW–Madison’s Space Science and Engineering Center track snow, ice and polar winds from a fleet of satellites. Their imagery lived in server folders sorted by satellite, instrument and date. As the design intern, I researched how they work and designed one platform to find, compare, animate and share it, built to take in new satellites without a redesign.

01 · Context

Climate research starts with being able to find the picture.

Satellites carry instruments that measure the poles every day. SSEC’s software turns those measurements into images of snow cover, sea-ice age and thickness, and polar winds, daily, weekly and monthly. Scientists watch them closely to spot change, and share them with collaborators around the world.

  • 3 variablesSnow, ice, polar winds
  • 2 regionsArctic and Antarctic
  • Many satellitesNOAA-20, NOAA-21, SNPP, Terra, Aqua…
  • Daily imageryAnd weekly and monthly composites

02 · Problem

The data was organised the way it was produced, not the way it was used.

Images landed in server directories by satellite, then instrument, then date. That mirrors the pipeline, but a scientist starts from a question about snow or ice. Every new satellite added another branch to dig through, and nothing in a folder could be compared, animated or shared without downloading it first.

03 · Research

I followed the data from the folder to the scientist’s inbox.

  1. 5 interviewsScientists who work with snow, ice and polar winds data every day
  2. Workflow studyThe current folders, scripts and email, mapped as a journey
  3. Competitive reviewNASA’s Eyes on the Earth and NSIDC’s Sea Ice Today

Every step of the old workflow cost time

I mapped how a lead scientist works today. Each stage started hopeful and ended in friction: digging through folders, opening images in other software to compare them, and zipping large files to email.

“It’s difficult to compare these files side by side.”Scientist, workflow mapping

The public tools were either beautiful or rigorous

Scientists needed both: the rigour of NSIDC and the exploration of NASA, in a tool built for daily work rather than public outreach.

04 · Insights

Scientists start from a variable, and end with a comparison.

The interviews kept circling the same four needs.

  1. They know the variable first

    Scientists arrive knowing they want snow, ice or polar winds. The satellite and instrument come after.

  2. Comparison is the real work

    Arctic against Antarctic, today against yesterday. Doing it across downloaded files was the biggest time sink.

  3. The image is the data

    They need full resolution and deep zoom to read patterns, not thumbnails.

  4. Research is shared

    Images go to colleagues worldwide. Downloading and sharing had to take one click, not a zip file.

How might wehelp scientists find, compare and share polar data across satellites, regions and time, without knowing where it’s stored?

05 · Decisions

Five decisions that shaped the platform.

I sketched every main screen, explored layout options in wireframes, and reviewed them with the scientists and developers each round. These were the calls that mattered most.

Decision 1 From insight 1

Organise by what scientists study, not what collects it.

Options
Mirror the folders (satellite, then instrument), or start from the variable.
Chose
Three doors: Snow, Ice and Polar Winds. Satellite, instrument and date become settings inside each one.
Why
It matches how scientists think, and it scales. Satellites launch and retire; snow and ice stay. A new satellite becomes an option, not a new branch.

Decision 2 From the folder structure

Turn the folder path into a panel of settings.

Tension
Scientists still need to pick a satellite, instrument and date. Hiding them would lose precision; nesting them recreates the folders.
Chose
One configuration panel beside the image: instrument, version, region, parameter and date, then Apply. The image stays in place.
Why
Every level of the old path is still there, but as a choice you can change without starting over.

Decision 3 From insight 2

Make comparison the default view, not a task.

Tension
Comparing meant downloading files and lining them up in other software, every time.
Chose
A comparison view built in: Arctic and Antarctic side by side, and the previous day directly beneath, with the variable explained alongside.
Why
Region across, time down: the two questions scientists ask most, answered in one screen.

Decision 4 From insight 3

Put time in motion, and history in a grid.

Tension
Climate change is change over time, but a folder shows one day at a time.
Chose
Playback for each product, with Loop and Rock modes and speed control, plus a grid of the last 15 days for snow and winds.
Why
Animation shows movement, like sea ice drifting. The grid shows the overall trend. Scientists read both.

Decision 5 From insights 3 and 4

Let the image fill the screen, with the tools on its edge.

Tension
Scientists need deep zoom and quick sharing, but panels of controls would shrink the very image they study.
Chose
A slim toolbar on every image: details, download, share, zoom and full screen, with arrows to step through days.
Why
The data stays as large as possible, and sharing with a colleague is a click, not a zip file and an email.

06 · Craft

Calm enough for daily work, built to grow.

  • A quiet palette

    Cornflower blue for snow and ice, ming green and violet as accents, and Inter for legibility, so the colourful data maps stay the loudest thing on screen.

  • Components and variants

    Every card, control and panel built as Figma components, so a new product is assembled, not designed from scratch.

  • Sketch to annotated handoff

    Paper sketches, then wireframes annotated for the developers, explaining each panel, control and interaction.

07 · Outcome

The blueprint for SSEC’s products platform.

The final designs became the basis for development and were featured in SSEC’s internal presentations. Scientists can now start from the variable they study, compare regions and days in one view, watch change over time, and share an image in a click. Adding a new satellite means adding an option, not redesigning the site.

  • 1platform in place of server folders
  • 0downloads needed to compare or share
  • Scalablenew satellites become settings

08 · Reflection

What I’d take to the next project.

  1. Architecture is a design decision. The most useful thing I did wasn’t a screen. It was choosing to sort by variable instead of by satellite, which made every screen after it simpler.
  2. I’d test with scientists, not just review with them. Team reviews shaped the design, but watching a scientist use the prototype would have found problems that conversation can’t.
  3. Next, accessibility and wider comparison. Rainbow colour maps are hard for colour-blind readers, so I’d offer accessible palettes alongside keyboard and screen-reader support. Then comparison across more than two datasets.
