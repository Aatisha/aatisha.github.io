Making 32,000 research papers easy to find, and easy to add to.

MINDS@UW is the open-access research repository for the University of Wisconsin System. As its sole designer, I turned an archive shaped around its administrators into a front door for researchers on every UW campus, and shipped it.

01 · Context

A repository is only as useful as what people find in it, and put into it.

Researchers across the UW System deposit their theses, datasets and papers in MINDS@UW so the world can find, cite and build on them. It held a wealth of research, but people described it as hard to navigate and harder to contribute to.

  • 32,000 itemsTheses, papers, reports
  • 3,000 usersResearchers, faculty, admins
  • 500+ collectionsHumanities to agriculture
  • UW SystemMadison, Milwaukee, Eau Claire, Stout and more

02 · Problem

Thousands of papers, and no clear way in.

The homepage was a sitemap: a long list of policies and campus links, and nothing that said what you could do here. People who came to share their work couldn't find where to start, and people who came to read it hit closed collections with no explanation.

“It’s not immediately clear how to start a submission.”Researcher

03 · Research

I listened to everyone the platform served, not just the people who ran it.

  1. Competitive reviewDukeSpace, Syracuse’s SURFACE and Cornell’s eCommons
  2. 9 interviewsLibrarians, department admins, researchers and first-time users
  3. Affinity mapping170+ notes clustered into 7 themes and 4 insights

Three groups, competing needs

Mapping who MINDS served showed the tension early. The platform was tuned for its power users, while its main contributors and an entire group of campuses were working around it.

  • Power usersAdminsCreate collections, mint DOIs, edit records and pull usage reports.
  • Main contributorsUW–Madison researchersLibrarians, department admins and researchers who deposit the work.
  • OverlookedUW System campusesLibrary teams outside Madison who curate their own campus’s collections.

Peers polished search, and buried depositing

Other repositories looked clean and put search up front. But the one job a repository exists for, adding work to it, was hidden, inconsistent or open only to some users. Only eCommons made it obvious. That set the bar: contributing had to be as easy to find as searching.

04 · Insights

MINDS was designed around the people who run it, not the people who use it.

Different people were doing the same tasks through different, undocumented paths. Yet everyone wanted MINDS to succeed. It needed to grow from an administrative archive into a public space for research.

  1. Navigation was a guessing game

    Users clicked through layer after layer to reach a single item, and couldn’t see where to start a deposit.

  2. Campuses outside Madison felt left out

    System librarians saw a Madison tool with their names in a list, not a shared home for their work.

    It is hard to push on our campus because it looks very Madison.
  3. The value was invisible

    Researchers couldn’t see what MINDS did for their work: who read it, and where it travelled.

  4. Closed collections were dead ends

    Access ran through library loans that no one explained, so requests turned into email chains.

How might wemake MINDS@UW feel like it belongs to every researcher in the UW System, without losing the depth its administrators rely on?

05 · Decisions

Five decisions that shaped the redesign.

I had a wall of ideas and limited engineering time. I prioritised what users needed most against what the platform could support, and kept the UW visual language so the change felt like an upgrade, not a stranger.

Decision 1

Lead with two jobs, not a sitemap.

First design
A homepage centred on search and campus identity, like most peers.
Chose
Two clear actions above the fold, Make a deposit and Browse communities, with search kept in the bar.
Why
Search already worked. Depositing was the job people couldn’t find, and the one that keeps a repository alive.
Before After

Decision 2

Give every campus a face, not a line in a list.

First try
Campuses grouped in a cleaner sitemap.
Testing showed
System users still didn’t see themselves, or what each campus had contributed.
Chose
A communities page of campus cards: a photo, what each holds, and one step into its collections.

Decision 3

Show where research comes from, and where it goes.

Tension
Item pages were walls of text. Readers needed to trust a source; authors wanted proof of reach.
Chose
Structured metadata, files up front, and usage analytics with the item’s history on every page.
Why
Provenance builds trust for readers, and visible impact is the reason authors deposit again.

Decision 4

Fix the conversation, not just the screen.

Tension
Closed collections looked like a UI problem. The root cause was manual back-and-forth between users and librarians.
Chose
An access-management workflow for admins: one request queue, automatic responses and reusable message templates.
Why
A clearer notice alone would still end in an email chain. Designing the admin side is what made the public side work.

Decision 5

Build analytics beside the repository, not inside it.

Constraint
A feasibility review with engineers ruled out building the dashboards into the repository itself.
Chose
A separate, hosted analytics service, designed with data analysts and librarians around the data that existed.
Result
Impact reports now take 65% less time to produce, and campuses can show what they contribute.

06 · Craft

The details that hold it together.

  • A design system

    Reusable components, a palette and a type scale built within the UW brand, so every page reads as one product.

  • Accessible by default

    Tested against WCAG with BrowserStack, from text contrast to colour-blindness simulations on key pages.

  • Make a deposit Browse collections How to deposit
    Words that point to tasks

    I wrote the copy, from task-first buttons to error messages, and refined it with the team over several rounds.

07 · Outcome

Shipped, and already doing its job.

The redesign is live for the whole UW System. Usability testing shaped it before handoff, and its first measured win came from the analytics service.

  • 65%faster impact reporting, measured
  • ~50%fewer support requests, projected
  • Every campuswith its own page and collections

08 · Reflection

What I’d take to the next project.

  1. Design for the quiet users. The campuses outside Madison never complained the loudest, but their absence was the clearest signal in the research.
  2. Often, the fix is a workflow. Much of the friction lived in emails and handoffs, not pixels. Designing for admins made the experience better for everyone.
  3. Next, I’d measure the deposit. With it live, the metric I’d watch is deposit completion, and how many first-time contributors come back.
